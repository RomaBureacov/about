Artificial Intelligence (or Large Language Models)

This subsection is dedicated just as a discussion on the use of large language models (LLM) which are commonly referred to as AI. I mainly wanted to bring this up since I am oriented towards computer science. I find this technology to definitely be a curious one, but of course as with anything it has its downsides. I do not entirely condemn the use of this technology, and I see it only ever grow like a slime mold.

My stance is that LLMs are a perfect way to get information fast and easy, it's a great tool for asking questions that a search engine might not be able to satisfy. But LLMs are just that, they're useful tools. Part of my independent studies made use of LLMs, as sometimes there are better solutions to problems that you are not aware of or wouldn't find easily; however I do not recommend simply copy-pasting such solutions. The entire art of learning how to do something and eventually apply it is through practice. You can't just ignore textbooks because they're large, bulky, and dense; the entire reason they exist is to teach you how to be good at something!

In short: I use LLMs myself to get information, but I don't use it as a substitute for development. They're useful tools, part of being a good programmer after all is knowing how to look up a solution to your particular problem you're stuck on.

  • TCSS 421 - Compiler Construction

    University of Washington - Tacoma

    Academic Fall Quarter 2025

    Textbook: Introduction to Compiler Construction in a Java World (ISBN-13: 978-1439860885)

    This class was about the process of making a compiler. We used an already mostly constructed compiler to learn about the details of a compiler. The compiler was for a language named J-- (J-minus-minus) and was a subset of Java, written in Java, targeting the Java virtual machine.

    Here we got some practice on using a grammar, and learned that compilation occurs in three distinct stages: parsing, analyze, codegen; the parser of the compiler makes use of a scanner, whose job is to read the input, character by character, and recognize tokens. The parser asks the scanner for tokens and interprets the tokens based on what it has already seen. After parsing, it analyzes what it has parsed and performs semantic routines (such as if it's legal to add a boolean to an int). If the analysis stage succeeds, it performs codegen.

    For additional context to the above paragraph, this all happens in an Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) (really a graph, as some nodes might relate to more than one parent). The parser makes the AST with the help of the scanner, then the compiler will run down the AST to analyze what it has, and finally it runs down the AST again to perform codegen; codegen is where the actual code that the computer will understand, such as assembly or some intermediate representation, is output.

  • MGMT 450 - Managing Work Teams

    University of Washington - Tacoma

    Academic Spring Quarter 2026

    Textbooks:

    • Teams That Work: The Seven Drivers of Team Effectiveness (ISBN-13: 978-0197764596)
    • Dream Teams: Working Together Without Falling Apart (ISBN-13: 978-0735217799)

    This class teaches how to make effective teams. It is a business class and is intended for management majors, but I took it as one of my electives as a computer scientist.

    Some of my favorite parts from this course:

    • Google did a study on what made effective teams, and they discovered that one of the biggest factors (also mentioned in Teams That Work) was Psychological Safety , which can be defined as the perception that if you as a team member will speak up to provide a dissenting opinion or input, you believe you will not be ostracized, reprimanded, or judged.
    • Hiring more "stars" doesn't scale: more stars for highly interdependent teams can actually lead to less effective teams.
    • Emotional contagion is this idea that one person can infect other people with their emotions. This is most significant in the aspect that "workplace rudeness can spread" (Teams That Work).
    • When is comes to how familiar team members are with each other: how familiar they are with each other professionally (such as skills) is more important than how familiar they are with each other personally (such as life outside of work).
    • There is a good quote from Teams That Work: you cannot "control" [trust], you can only "influence" it... You can't make someone trust you, but you can act in a manner that increases the likelihood that they will
      In addition, there was the idea of trust: Trust is a business imperative. The important thing to note here is that trust and psychological safety are two sides of the same coin, trust is about you trusting the other person, and psychological safety is about you believing that the others trust you.
    • Team debriefs are shown to improve team efficiency: the reason being that it provides the means for team members to reflect and learn from what they did or did not do. If you manage a team, consider team debriefs!
    • Another good quote from Teams That Work: Purpose and identity can help a team succeed.
      One factor that helps a team's working conditions is being able to understand what their purpose actually is. If members do not understand, then your team may itself work inefficiently as they don't know what they're actually supposed to do.

    This class is still in progress, so I can't expand on it fully, but do ask if you're curious.

  • Split Keyboard

    Started towards the end of Summer of 2024

    Solo Project

    GitHub Link

    I had assembled the Elora Rev1 staggered-column, ortholinear split keyboard after some light research in the end of Summer 2024. The keyboard makes use of the open-source Quantum Mechanical Keyboard (QMK) firmware, written in C.

    I call this a project because it is an ongoing experiment with infrequent updates. The "experiment" part of it is the keymaps and various features offered from QMK. There is no one correct keymap, rather there are many good alternative keymaps, and sometimes it's better to make a layer over the current keys that is dedicated to, say, the number pad.

    The features from QMK I've implemented into the keyboard are layers, key combos, and the music mode. It is my daily-driver, and while I am still capable of using a traditional staggered-row, full keyboard, my preference is now always this very split keyboard: the reason being it is much more ergonomic, making for a drastically better computer experience.

  • 3D Rendering Demo

    Academic Fall Quarter 2025

    Solo Project

    GitHub Link

    This project was part of the Matrix (Linear) Algebra course I took: we were supposed to find and present an application on matrix algebra. I was curious about 3D rendering, so I delved into learning how to actually make a basic 3D renderer, whose purpose was to showcase the process of making a 3D world into a 2D image (minus shading as that was outside of scope).

    The project was written from scratch using Java and Oracle's Swing library. The source I used to learn the theory and how to actually make it was from scratchapixel. Tests for matrix math was done in JUnit.

    This project also asked of me to implement algorithms for matrix multiplication and matrix inversion. Matrix multiplication was done in the standard O(n^3) fashion, whereas the matrix inversion was done via the method of an adjoint matrix and the determinant (see: pages 115-116 of Linear Algebra with Applications, ISBN-13: 978-1292354866). It should be noted that this matrix inversion algorithm I devised required finding the cofactor; it was an O(n!) algorithm (where n is the size of the matrix), you would want to be careful using it when n > 6!

    If you would like to hear my informal lecture on this topic, I would be happy to provide (though do note I could talk about this for easily an hour).
