Artificial Intelligence (or Large Language Models)

This subsection is dedicated just as a discussion on the use of large language models (LLM) which are commonly referred to as AI. I mainly wanted to bring this up since I am oriented towards computer science. I find this technology to definitely be a curious one, but of course as with anything it has its downsides. I do not entirely condemn the use of this technology, and I see it only ever grow like a slime mold.

My stance is that LLMs are a perfect way to get information fast and easy, it's a great tool for asking questions that a search engine might not be able to satisfy. But LLMs are just that, they're useful tools. Part of my independent studies made use of LLMs, as sometimes there are better solutions to your programming problems that you are not exactly aware of and wouldn't find easily, however I do not recommend simply copy-pasting such programming solutions. The entire art of learning how to do something and eventually apply it is through practice. You can't just ignore textbooks because they're large, bulky, and dense; the entire reason they exist is to teach you how to be good at something!

In short: I use LLMs myself to get information, but I don't use it as a substitute for development. They're useful tools, part of being a good programmer after all is knowing how to look up a solution to your particular problem you're stuck on.

  • TCSS 421 - Compiler Construction

    University of Washington - Tacoma

    Academic Fall Quarter 2025

    Textbook: Textbook

    This class was about the process of making a compiler. We used an already mostly constructed compiler to learn about the details of a compiler. The compiler was for a language named J-- (J-minus-minus) and was a subset of Java, written in Java, targeting the Java virtual machine.

    Here I learned what a grammar was, and that compilation occurs in three distinct stages: parsing, analyze, codegen; the parser of the compiler makes use of a scanner, whose job is to read the input, character by character, and recognize tokens. The parser asks the scanner for tokens and interprets the tokens based on what it has already seen. After parsing, it analyzes what it has parsed and performs semantic routines (such as if it's legal to add a boolean to an int). If the analyze stage succeeds, it performs codegen.

    For additional context to the above paragraph, this all happens in an Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) (really a graph, as some nodes might relate to more than one parent). The parser makes the AST with the help of the scanner, then the compiler will run down the AST to analyze what it has, and finally it runs down the AST again to perform codegen; codegen is where the actual code that the computer will understand, such as assembly, is output.
